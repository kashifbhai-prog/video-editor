Video editing for beginners: 15 terms you need to know

{{postMeta}}

Every craft has its own vocabulary, and video editing has a lot of it. Tutorials talk about "J-cuts", "B-roll" and "keyframes" as if everyone knew them. This glossary explains the fifteen terms you will meet most often, in plain language, with an example of how each one is used. Read it once and editing tutorials – and editing software – will make a lot more sense.

The workspace

1. Timeline

The timeline is the long area at the bottom of the editor where you build your video. Time runs from left to right: the left edge is the start of your video, and every clip you place there plays when the playhead reaches it. Editing is mostly arranging things on the timeline.

Example: to make your intro come before your main video, drag the intro clip to the left of it on the timeline.

2. Track (layer)

The timeline is divided into horizontal rows called tracks. Tracks higher up are drawn on top of tracks lower down – like sheets of paper on a desk. Video editors usually have a main video track, overlay tracks for things placed on top (logos, picture-in-picture, B-roll), text tracks and audio tracks.

Example: put your logo on an overlay track above the main video, and it appears in front of the video for as long as the logo clip lasts.

3. Clip

A clip is one piece of media on the timeline – a section of a video, a photo, a piece of music or a title. One video file from your phone can become many clips after you cut it up. Changing a clip on the timeline never changes the original file on your computer.

4. Playhead

The playhead is the vertical line that moves across the timeline during playback. It marks the current moment: the frame you see in the player. Many actions happen "at the playhead" – splitting, adding text, pasting.

Example: press ← and → to move the playhead one frame at a time when you need an exact position.

Basic edits

5. Trim

Trimming means shortening a clip from its start or its end, without cutting it into pieces. You drag the edge of the clip inwards – or, in Cutistic, put the playhead where the clip should start or end and press Q or W.

Example: you start talking two seconds after you pressed record. Trim the start so the clip begins on your first word.

6. Split (cut)

Splitting divides one clip into two at the playhead (S). You then have two separate clips you can move, delete or change independently. Two splits around a mistake, then deleting the middle piece, is the most common edit there is.

7. Jump cut

A jump cut is a cut between two moments of the same shot, so the subject seems to "jump" slightly. In talking-head videos, jump cuts are how pauses and mistakes are removed. They can be used as a deliberate, energetic style – or softened by zooming in slightly on every other clip (a "punch-in").

Learn more in How to edit talking-head videos.

8. B-roll

The main footage – usually a person speaking – is called A-roll. B-roll is everything you cut to while the voice continues: close-ups, screen recordings, photos, scenery. It shows what the speaker describes and hides cuts in the A-roll.

Example: while you say "first, chop the onions", the viewer sees a close-up of your hands chopping onions, placed on an overlay track.

9. J-cut and L-cut

These are cuts where the sound and the picture change at different moments. In a J-cut, you hear the next scene before you see it. In an L-cut, the picture changes but the sound of the previous scene continues. Both make edits feel smooth and natural, the way films and interviews do. (The names come from the shape the clips make on an old-fashioned timeline.)

How: select a video clip and choose Extract audio. The sound moves to its own audio track, so you can trim the picture and the sound to different lengths.

Movement and style

10. Keyframe

A keyframe records the value of a setting at a moment in time. With two keyframes – a start value and an end value – the editor animates the setting smoothly between them. Keyframes are how slow zooms, sliding logos and music that fades down under speech are made.

Example: put the playhead at the start of a photo, click the ◇ next to Scale (100%). Move the playhead to the end and set Scale to 115%. The photo now zooms in slowly. The Keyframes section lets you change the easing – for example Ease in-out for a softer start and stop.

11. Transition

A transition is an effect between two clips: a fade, a wipe, a slide, a zoom. Used sparingly, transitions signal a change of place or time. Used on every cut, they look amateurish – most professional videos use plain cuts nearly everywhere and save transitions for real changes of scene.

Technical settings

12. Aspect ratio

The shape of the picture, written as width:height. 16:9 is landscape (YouTube, TV), 9:16 is tall and vertical (Shorts, Reels, TikTok), 1:1 is square, and 4:5 is a slightly tall portrait used in Instagram and Facebook feeds. Set it at the start of a project, because everything you place on screen is positioned within that shape.

13. Resolution

The number of pixels in the picture. 1080p (Full HD) is 1920 × 1080 for landscape video; 4K is 3840 × 2160. Higher resolution can show more detail, but only if the original footage has that detail. For most online video, 1080p is the sweet spot.

14. Frame rate

How many still pictures make up one second of video, written as fps (frames per second). 24 fps gives a "cinema" look, 25 and 30 fps are standard for most video, and 50 or 60 fps makes fast movement smoother. Keep the frame rate you filmed at when you export.

15. Bitrate and codec

Video files are compressed, otherwise a single minute would take gigabytes. The codec is the method of compression – H.264 plays almost everywhere, HEVC (H.265) makes smaller files but is not supported by every device. The bitrate is how much data is used per second of video: higher bitrate means better quality and bigger files. Our guide to the best export settings explains how to choose both.

Bonus: export (render)

Exporting – also called rendering – is the final step: the editor plays through your whole timeline, combines every track, effect and title into single frames, compresses them and writes an MP4 file you can upload or share. Until you export, your "video" is really just a list of instructions.

Where to go from here

The best way to learn these terms is to use them. Open the editor, import a short clip from your phone and try a split, a trim, a keyframe zoom and an export – it takes about ten minutes.
